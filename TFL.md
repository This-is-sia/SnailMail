# Engineering a City That Can’t Stop

*How Transport for London evolves a complex system while keeping it running*

## 1. A system that cannot stop

Transport for London (TfL) runs one of the most complex transport systems in the world. Millions of journeys a day depend on trains, buses, stations, roads, ticketing systems, control rooms and digital services, much of which has evolved over decades and was never designed to work together.

TfL cannot switch a service off while it rebuilds it, and a failure does not mean an unavailable feature but stranded passengers, a disrupted city and, sometimes, a safety risk. Yet standing still is not an option either. Demand grows, assets age, and passengers expect contactless payment, live data and a phone signal in the tunnels. TfL has had to change a system while it runs, which raises a practical question: how do you modernise something you cannot afford to stop?

The record is uneven, and the reason is instructive. Because TfL cannot afford to fail, it changes the system in ways that keep each step survivable: decisions are made locally so one fault cannot stop a station, commitments such as pricing are deferred until it is safe to make them, fragile systems are shielded behind new layers, and new capabilities run in parallel or in shadow before anyone relies on them. These techniques are cheap in software, where a stage can be paused, rolled back or run alongside the old one. They are expensive in physical infrastructure, where a cable in a live tunnel cannot be trialled and undone, which is why the same organisation can move steadily in some places and slowly in others. The techniques also leave complexity behind, because surviving a change is not the same as simplifying a system.

This essay follows that idea through the episodes that define TfL’s recent history, from open data and the cloud to Oyster and contactless, the Citymapper bus, mobile coverage underground and the move into AI. Each is judged by what it achieved and what it cost, and compared where possible with New York, Paris and other cities, because TfL’s position owes as much to where it started as to how it has performed. The lessons are drawn together at the end.

## 2. Shielding the core: open data and the cloud

Two of TfL’s most effective changes share a pattern: neither required it to replace, or put at risk, the systems that run the network.

The first was open data. By publishing live bus arrivals, timetables and journey-planning data through APIs, TfL allowed outside developers to build their own services without needing to understand the systems behind them. That drew a useful line between the systems that operate transport and the applications through which customers experience it, and it let innovation happen beyond TfL’s boundaries with nothing for TfL to build or maintain. I saw a small version of this myself: my brother built an Arduino widget that used TfL’s APIs to tell us when buses were due at the two stops closest to us. Because the data flows outwards, a flaw in a developer’s app cannot reach the railway. It was also staged: embeddable widgets in 2007, a developer area in 2009, a London datastore in 2010, live bus arrivals in 2011 and an API for them in 2012, and by 2013 more than 5,000 developers had registered. Staging did not prevent every problem. In July 2010 TfL had to halt its newly introduced API feed because it was unprepared for sudden demand, and a routine security update later that year interrupted the data supply. The Tube departures feed was not restored until December 2010, about half a year after it was suspended, and then on Microsoft’s Azure cloud platform, built to handle millions of requests a day, an early sign of why a shielding layer mattered.

UK Open Banking applies the same principle at national scale: a standardised framework through which authorised third parties can interact with banks. By 2026 it had passed one billion cumulative payments and 100 billion API calls, though that does not mean every competitive benefit has followed, so the analogy is about architecture rather than outcomes. The differences are real, since transport data is largely public while financial data involves personal information and explicit consent, but the shared point stands: a well-designed interface lets others innovate without unrestricted access to what lies beneath.

The second was the cloud. TfL’s website and APIs must cope with surges in traffic, especially during disruption, when passengers all look for information at once. Yet some of the back-end systems supplying that information could not cope with equivalent volumes, and rebuilding them was not practical. In one account of TfL’s AWS-based platform, the API layer handled around 10 million calls a day while some back-end components could process only a small number of queries per minute. The platform therefore sat between the public and those systems, absorbing and aggregating demand, scaling independently and shielding the components underneath. TfL describes its unified API in just these terms, as a mediator and façade between the people using it and the core source systems, so that those systems can change without breaking the apps built on top. The website had been running on AWS for over a year by September 2015. The important change was not moving technology into the cloud but introducing a layer that could take the unpredictability of modern digital traffic so that the legacy systems did not have to.

Both approaches also had a further advantage: they could be staged, paused and reversed, as the 2010 halt shows, and they needed little from the physical railway. There were no engineering windows to book, no space to find in stations and no other operators to coordinate with. What they did not do is remove the complexity beneath them. A cloud layer cannot resolve unclear ownership, an API cannot guarantee that data is accurate or consistently interpreted, and TfL still has to understand every dependency between the new platform and the legacy services behind it. Wrapping buys flexibility; it does not buy simplicity.

## 3. Deciding locally, pricing later: from Oyster to contactless

Architecture is often a record of the constraints an organisation has had to solve, and Oyster is a clear example. Introduced in the early 2000s, it had to process journeys quickly at busy barriers, reaching a decision in a fraction of a second, without waiting on a network, before a passenger was let through. Networks in the early 2000s were also unlikely to return a reliable answer for every tap within the response time a barrier requires, a limit TfL has since put at a maximum acceptable transaction time of around 500 milliseconds. Much of the fare logic was therefore distributed to the readers, which processed interactions locally while the wider system reconciled journey information afterwards.

In practice, the card itself stored the traveller’s balance or ticket, and each reader held the fare rules. On every tap the reader checked the card, worked out the fare and any capping, and wrote the new balance back to the card, with no call to a central system. Readers then sent their transaction records to the back office in batches, so the back office held a record of journeys already completed. The design was resilient and scalable, but it fixed assumptions that later changes would have to work around.

Those assumptions became costs. Because the balance and fare rules lived on the card and in the readers, Oyster was a closed system that only London used. A TfL presentation on future ticketing listed the consequences: passengers had to manage pay-as-you-go balances and season tickets themselves, and many visitors did not have a card at all. A visitor unfamiliar with Oyster had to find a visitor centre or station machine to buy one, which could mean significant delays. Keeping fare logic in the readers also meant maintaining it across all of them. The barrier’s time limit had not gone away, but contactless offered a way to meet it without a TfL-issued card.

Contactless faced the same time limit but solved it differently. Because a bank card carries no TfL balance to update, the reader no longer needs to work out the fare at the barrier: it checks the card and records the tap, and a central system later pieces taps into journeys, calculates the fare and charges the bank card, using existing bank-card infrastructure. Passengers already carried the credential, so TfL no longer had to issue and top up its own card, and fare calculation and capping could move into a back-office system that also supported proper online account management, with less fare logic to maintain across individual readers. It was a genuine success: TfL introduced a new payment method across a large network without asking passengers to learn a new way of travelling, and without replacing every station, bus or operational system. But a bank card is a payment credential, not a travel entitlement. Some season-ticket and concession products remain tied to specific ticketing mechanisms, and weekly capping runs over a fixed Monday-to-Sunday week, whereas a seven-day Travelcard can start on any day. The system has to keep payment credentials, entitlements, journeys and fare rules distinct, so the simple tap conceals a hybrid.

TfL introduced contactless in stages, and alongside Oyster rather than instead of it. Acceptance began on buses in December 2012, a pilot with around 5,000 customers ran on the Tube and rail network from April 2014, and acceptance launched across the wider network in September 2014, under two years after the first buses. Oyster stayed in service throughout, which is one reason the fare system is a hybrid today.

Two other cities show how much the starting point matters. New York’s MTA launched its contactless system, OMNY, in 2019 and ended MetroCard sales at the close of 2025, completing a transition London has made only partly. It could go further for two reasons. Its subway and bus fare is largely flat, so the system never has to pair taps in and out to price a journey by zone, time of day and mode. And OMNY’s cap is a rolling seven-day window beginning with the first paid ride, which is closer to how a Travelcard behaves and removes one reason to keep a separate product. New York also adopted contactless later, after the bank-card model had been proven elsewhere, London included.

Paris took a third path. Île-de-France had relied on its Navigo pass and mobile ticketing, and has only recently begun adding card payment, through terminals on buses since 2025. The operator frames this as a fallback for occasional visitors, with higher fares and no connections included, and stresses that it does not replace existing fare options. Validation by bank card in the metro itself is reportedly due to follow from 2027.

London made bank cards the default and kept legacy products at the edges; New York is retiring its closed-loop card; Paris is keeping its pass ecosystem and adding cards as a safety net. None of these is simply better. Each follows from a different history, and London’s hybrid is as much a product of its Oyster base and zonal fares as of its choices. The episode also shows what digitising one interaction does and does not do: the visible interface became simpler, while the rules and products behind it stayed complicated. A transformation should be judged by how well it manages the complexity that remains.

## 4. Opening the edge is easier than changing the core: the Citymapper experiment

TfL’s open data made it easy for external developers to build on the network. Apps such as Citymapper combine TfL’s data with other sources to plan journeys and compare routes, adding real value without operating a train, bus or station. The picture changed when Citymapper tried to move from information to operations with its own bus service.

TfL rejected the application for the CM1 route. It cited congestion and the effect on other road users, the fact that the area was already well served, and the confusion a separate ticketing arrangement would cause by sitting outside TfL’s fare capping. It also considered the regulatory framework for demand-responsive buses too underdeveloped at the time. A sceptic could add that protecting fare capping and incumbent routes is itself a choice, and that a transport authority has an interest in the shape of its own network. But the objections were about system effects rather than technology: a new bus service has to fit with routes, capacity, fares, accessibility and the road space everyone else uses.

Citymapper then launched Smart Ride, an eight-seat, pre-booked service that worked more like shared private hire, a category that can operate within an existing licensing framework. It closed in 2019 after regulatory, commercial and strategic difficulties. The episode is not evidence that digital innovation in transport cannot work. It shows that the digital boundary of a system can be made permeable cheaply, because a failure there cannot reach the network, while the core is a shared public asset where a failure, or even an extra service, affects everyone who uses it. Freedom to innovate at the edge does not extend to changing the system itself.

## 5. Why physical change is slow: mobile coverage underground

The desired outcome is simple: reliable 4G and 5G in stations and tunnels. Delivering it means installing equipment inside an operating railway built long before mobile networks existed.

The delivery model is a neutral host. In June 2021 TfL awarded Boldyn Networks, formerly BAI Communications, a 20-year concession to build and run shared infrastructure that all the mobile operators use. The operators pay for equipment in tunnels and passenger areas, while TfL funds some back-office areas. That separates the physical network from the retail service and avoids each operator installing its own kit in the same cramped spaces.

The model settled a commercial problem that had held things up for years: trials planned around 2008 stalled while talks with infrastructure providers continued. The physical problems then took over. The build has been hampered by the age of the tunnels and the listed buildings above them. Work happens largely in overnight engineering windows, competing for access with maintenance and other projects, and the equipment shares space with signalling, power and communications systems. Because the tunnels are critical infrastructure, each is fitted with two leaky-feeder cables so that one can fail without losing coverage, which doubles what has to be installed. At the other extreme, the Elizabeth line was covered end to end by December 2024, with 4G and 5G in its stations and tunnels, which suggests how much the age of the older tunnels matters. A solution that works in one station or tunnel is not always transferable to the next.

The result has been slow. The original target was the end of 2024. By early 2026, more than three years into the build, only 62 of the 121 stations classed as properly underground had coverage. By October 2026 the figure had reached around 70 per cent, with TfL and the Mayor still aiming to finish by the end of the year.

Other cities got there first. Tokyo declared full coverage on its metro in 2013, Paris in 2020, and Berlin’s U-Bahn reached full 4G in 2024, so even on target London will be the last major Western peer to finish 4G. The delivery models differ. Madrid’s metro created its own neutral-host operator around the turn of the millennium, while Boldyn also works with New York’s MTA, in a system that runs around the clock but has some four-track lines where crews can work beside running trains. London’s tunnels are an honest confound here, since few systems are older or tighter. And coverage is not quality: Ookla found parts of the Tube now rival the streets above for 5G availability and signal strength, but it measured coverage and signal strength, not speeds, latency or capacity.

The tempting conclusion is that physical work is simply slower than digital work. The evidence points to something more specific: how cheaply a step can be undone. Contactless reached the whole network within two years of staged rollout, with a pilot and Oyster running alongside, and the open data feed could simply be paused when demand overwhelmed it. A cable in a live tunnel can be neither trialled alongside the old system nor easily removed, and each installation needs its own access window and safety case, shared with other parties and funders. The rollout is staged too, station by station, but each stage is a construction project rather than a software release, and a commercial model had to be settled first, which took years.

## 6. Staging AI where errors are costly

TfL’s interest in AI long predates generative models. A 2018 account describes data scientists and reliability analysts building a production project on the Central line to predict when a motor would fail, with expected annual savings of around £3 million, a forecast reported during the project rather than a confirmed outcome. The hard part was not the algorithm but the data: records drawn from several sources were incomplete or inconsistent, and rare failures left few examples to learn from. This is a common pattern in applied AI: the quality of the underlying data often limits a model more than the choice of algorithm. The Smart Stations pilot at Willesden Green took the wrapping approach to computer vision, adding analytics to existing CCTV to flag potential incidents for staff rather than replacing the cameras, though camera position, image quality and conditions all affected accuracy.

New York’s MTA offers a parallel. Its TrackInspect pilot mounted Google Pixel smartphones on the axles of select A line cars and ran on six cars from September 2024 to January 2025, collecting 335 million sensor readings. TfL built a model around existing event and maintenance records for a specific asset; the MTA generated new data with cheap consumer hardware. The MTA also added generative AI so inspectors can ask conversational questions about maintenance history and repair standards.

TfL’s main generative AI use so far is similarly bounded. For the Blackwall and Silvertown tunnel discount schemes, Azure OpenAI technology helps check whether submitted documents match the required name, address and vehicle details. According to TfL’s data protection assessment, applications that cannot be verified are referred for human review rather than rejected solely by the AI. Capita, the supplier, reports processing in under a minute against up to five days manually, and 29 automated fraud checks; those are supplier figures for the document-checking stage, not the whole application. The point is the shape of the use: identifiable inputs and outputs and a clear escalation path, rather than a chatbot or an attempt to automate a whole function.

Around it TfL has been building organisational foundations. A July 2025 update described an AI Direction Group for guidance and policy, a pilot Central Data Hub to help teams manage data, and an AI Lab to test proofs of concept. None of these is a finished enterprise data platform or evidence of production deployment, and the harder step is turning a proof of concept into a changed process with someone accountable for it. The same update acknowledged that TfL needs to strengthen its data foundations and develop its workforce, which is a sober admission. Data produced by teams for specific operational purposes is hard for others to interpret or combine, because definitions differ, records are incomplete and the context sits with the people who created it. The Hub and Lab try to build capability across existing teams rather than centralise everything, which suits an organisation whose operational knowledge is distributed, though it leaves the balance between local ownership and organisation-wide consistency still to be struck. AI adds a cultural problem on top, because its outputs can look authoritative even when incomplete or wrong. Governance, training and human oversight are therefore part of the operating model, not compliance added afterwards.

The stakes rise in safety-critical settings. In December 2025 TfL began a trial on the Central line at West Ruislip, East Acton and Mile End, combining existing cameras with new video analytics to detect people falling or entering the track area. It started with a shadow phase, the same idea as the contactless pilot, in which the system can identify potential incidents without triggering any operational response. That matters because a model that performs well in testing can behave differently amid changing light, crowds and passing trains, and because both errors cost something: false alarms burden staff and erode trust, while missed events defeat the purpose.

New York shows why. Its 2014 to 2019 pilots of lasers, thermal cameras and microwave scanners could identify an intrusion but, an MTA official said, not precisely enough to manage the response. It is now testing an AI-supported system at two stations, motivated in part by intrusions accounting for 6% of all subway delays, and has named alarm volume threatening on-time performance, staffing and funding as obstacles. Its 2024 weapons-detector pilot is a warning about false positives: more than 3,000 searches at 20 stations produced 12 knives, no guns and over 100 false alarms. The MTA is also piloting platform screen doors at three stations, having found significant constraints on a systemwide rollout, so barriers and detection are being treated as complements. A shadow phase is how TfL can learn a system’s false-positive rate before staff depend on it, and the design of alerts, priorities and responses is part of the system as much as the model is.

## 7. Conclusion: what TfL’s approach shows

TfL’s history is not a neat progression from legacy to modern. Oyster did not disappear when contactless arrived, contactless did not simplify the fare system behind it, and the cloud layer shields legacy dependencies without removing them. It would be easy to read that story as a success and equally easy to read it as one of delay; neither survives a careful look.

The strengths are real. Contactless gave London a payment model that other cities have since followed or are now adopting. The open data ecosystem lets third parties build products TfL would otherwise have to run itself. And its AI work has so far been introduced with unusual care: bounded use cases, human review and shadow phases.

The weaknesses are real too. Mobile coverage underground arrived late, and later than planned, leaving the Tube behind its peers. The fare system remains a hybrid that passengers have to understand. TfL’s case against Citymapper rests on system-level reasoning that is defensible but also protects the incumbent. And several of the AI benefits quoted in public are forecasts or supplier claims, not independent evaluations.

Fair comparison needs care in both directions. The Tube is the oldest metro system in the world, and few systems have tunnels as old and tight. New York’s flat fare made its contactless transition simpler, and it benefited from adopting after London had proved the model. At the same time, it might be expected that long experience of its own infrastructure would make TfL better at changing it, and the fact that the physical programmes are where it has struggled most suggests experience helps less than hoped. Knowing exactly what can go wrong does not create space in a tunnel or settle who pays for the equipment.

The pattern is nonetheless consistent. TfL has moved steadily where a step can be staged, paused and reversed, and slowly where it cannot, and the slowness owes more to how change is organised and funded than to a lack of skill. Six lessons follow.

1. **Make every step survivable.** Local decisions, deferred commitments, parallel running and shadow phases let TfL change a system it cannot stop, and the same logic lets it test AI before anyone relies on it.
2. **TfL wraps before it replaces.** Interfaces and intermediary layers have bought flexibility, but the complexity beneath them stays and still has to be understood.
3. **Starting positions shape outcomes.** Oyster’s design shaped contactless, and New York’s flat fare and Paris’s pass ecosystem show why a peer’s outcome cannot simply be copied.
4. **Physical change cannot be undone cheaply.** It cannot be trialled alongside the old system, so the commercial model, access windows and safety case need settling before the engineering starts.
5. **The edge opens more easily than the core.** Publishing data invites innovation; changing the network itself involves everyone who uses it.
6. **Wrapping has a price.** Incremental evolution is not free.

The last lesson is the one TfL’s record most clearly illustrates. A system that keeps gaining layers without ever paying down what lies beneath them will find its past decisions constraining its future ones. Technology creates possibilities; organisational capability, from data quality to clear ownership to the ability to act on what a model finds, decides whether they become lasting improvements.

For a system that cannot stop, the ability to evolve continuously is worth more than a perfect end state. But evolving continuously is not the same as evolving well. The lesson is not that complexity can be avoided; it is that it should be taken on deliberately, and paid down before it sets.
