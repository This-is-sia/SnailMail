A note before we start: I have never worked at TfL. This essay is my own interpretation of public material. While I tried to stick to independent evaluations; some figures come from suppliers. The thesis that TfL changes its systems in steps it can survive is my interpretation, not TfL's own account. I may well have got things wrong, and I'd welcome corrections from anyone who knows better.

1. A system that cannot stop
Transport for London (TfL) runs one of the oldest and most complex transport systems in the world. It's easy to think of it as just the Tube, but it is so much bigger than that: trains, buses, stations, roads, ticketing systems, control rooms and digital services, and a whole lot of other things under the surface. Not all of these components were there from day zero. Some were introduced at different points over the decades and forced to work together.
TfL can't switch itself off to rebuild. Think about how up in arms we Londoners get over Tube strikes or scheduled maintenance; it feels like the city throws a collective tantrum. But TfL also can't fail, because a catastrophic failure means stranded passengers or risks to their safety. And it can't stand still: demand keeps growing with London's population, it has ageing assets to manage, and it has a tough customer base that (tries to) hold it to a high standard. This tension interests me: how do you change a system safely while it keeps running?
TfL's road to modernisation is uneven, smooth in some places and rough in others, which makes it an interesting case study. My thesis is that TfL tries to keep each step survivable, meaning it can be paused and undone without causing a failure. It does this in four ways. It defers commitments where it can: the gate lets you through now and works out your fare later. It keeps each change as small as possible. It puts buffers in front of fragile systems to shield them. And it runs new capabilities in parallel or in shadow before anyone relies on them. These techniques are cheap in software, where a deployment can be paused, rolled back or run alongside the old one. They are expensive in physical infrastructure: a cable in a live tunnel is expensive to put in and expensive to take out. They also leave complexity behind (tech debt, if you will), because surviving a change is not the same as simplifying a system.
I'll look at these ideas through changes in TfL's history. Each is judged by what it achieved and what it cost and, where possible, compared with New York, Paris and other cities, because TfL's position is shaped not just by how it executed a change but by where it started.
2. Shielding the core: open data, the cloud and Citymapper
TfL's most effective changes share a pattern: they protected the core instead of changing it.
TfL opened its data through APIs, which let outsiders build on top of the network without touching the railway. Engineers could use TfL's data without affecting operations (as far as I can tell, the APIs were effectively read-only) and without having to understand how TfL worked underneath. That let innovation happen beyond TfL's boundaries, in services TfL didn't have to build or maintain. I saw this in person: in 2015 my brother built an Arduino widget that used the TfL APIs to tell us when buses were due at the two stops closest to us.
The journey was staged: embeddable widgets in 2007, a developer area in 2009, a London datastore in 2010, live bus arrivals in 2011 and an API for them in 2012, and by 2013 more than 5,000 registered developers. It was not all a smooth journey, though. In July 2010 TfL had to halt the live Tube feed because it wasn't prepared for the sudden demand, and a routine security update later that year interrupted some of the data supply. The feed was only restored in December 2010, once it was running on Microsoft's Azure cloud platform.
The edge only opens so far, though. Citymapper built a journey planner on TfL's data, but when it applied to run its own bus route, CM1, TfL said no. It cited congestion, an area already well served, and a separate ticketing arrangement that would sit outside TfL's fare capping. Citymapper then launched Smart Ride, an eight-seat pre-booked service closer to shared private hire, which closed in 2019. (A vague memory of this is actually what inspired this article.) A skeptic could say that protecting fare capping also protects the incumbent, but I read TfL's reasons as being about system effects, not technology. Publishing data can't change the railway, though as 2010 showed it can still overload TfL's own systems, which is why the feed needed a shield. Adding a bus to the road is a different order of risk.
Cloud migrations tend to follow two broad patterns: lift and shift, or rebuild natively for the cloud. TfL has done both. In January 2021 it moved its SAP back-office systems (finance, procurement and the like), 252 servers' worth according to the supplier, from a shared data centre to Amazon Web Services (AWS), which looks like a lift and shift. Its website went the other way: it looks like it was rebuilt for AWS over about two years and ran in parallel in beta for six months before a full launch in March 2014. The 2010 Tube feed shows a third pattern. TfL's servers kept producing the data while a façade on Azure handled the large volume of calls. Where it can, TfL moves a system as it is; where it can't, it puts a layer in front. The same idea was used later in the unified API. In one account of TfL's AWS-based platform, the API layer handled around 10 million calls a day while some back-end components could only process a small number of queries per minute. TfL describes the unified API as a façade between users and its core systems, which lets it scale to demand and absorb changes to the systems underneath.
Overall, open data and the façade could be staged, paused and reversed, as the 2010 halt shows. They needed little from the physical network, whether railway or road, so there were no engineering windows to book, no road space to share and no other operators to coordinate with. Citymapper's bus needed all of that, which is why TfL treated it so differently. However, what the digital changes didn't do is remove the complexity beneath them. A façade doesn't resolve unclear ownership, bad data or muddled dependencies. Wrapping buys flexibility; it does not buy simplicity.
3. Deciding locally, pricing later: Oyster to contactless
Architecture is often a record of the constraints an organisation has had to solve, and the way the Oyster works is a clear example. Introduced in the early 2000s, it had to process journeys quickly at busy barriers, deciding in a fraction of a second whether it was letting the passenger through. Networks in the early 2000s were unlikely to reliably return an answer for every tap within the SLA TfL put for acceptable transaction time (around 500 ms). This meant that the fare logic was distributed to each of the readers as they processed the interactions locally while the wider system reconciled journey information afterwards.
Let’s go into slightly more detail. The card stores the traveller's balance or ticket, while the reader holds the fare rules. Once the card is tapped on the reader, the reader checks the card, works out the fare and any capping and then writes the new balance back onto the card. There is no call to a central system. The readers then sent their transaction records to the back office in batches, so the back office held a record of journeys already completed (this is why you can look up on the TfL website where all your Oyster was tapped). This design is resilient and scalable with one fatal flaw: this is a closed system.
A TfL presentation on the future ticketing listed the consequences: passengers had to manage pay-as-you-go balances and season tickets themselves and many of London's tourists had no card at all. A visitor had to find a visitor centre or station machine to buy one which could mean significant delays not to mention the environmental waste of Oyster cards only used for the short period of holidaying. Another issue was that any updates to the fare logic had to be rolled out to all readers across London. These were among the reasons why contactless payment was introduced.
The contactless pattern met the same SLA set by TfL for acceptable transaction time but it was solved differently and rolled out in stages. The reader no longer has to work out the fare, it simply records the tap and a central system later pieces taps into journeys, calculates the fare and charges the bank card, using existing bank-card infrastructure. The readers no longer checked for sufficient balance or had to update the balance so the fare calculation could move to a back-office system. It was a genuine success: TfL introduced a new payment method across a large network without asking passengers to learn a new way of travelling and without replacing every station, bus or operational system. However, the bank card is a payment credential, not a travel entitlement. Season tickets and concessions products remain tied to specific ticketing mechanisms and weekly capping runs over fixed Monday to Sunday week, whereas a seven-day Travelcard can start on any day. In a way, this has introduced more complexity because your visitor has to realise these intricacies and know when to switch to an Oyster card or paper ticket.
Now let's compare TfL's contactless rollout with New York's MTA and Paris' Île-de-France. MTA launched its contactless system, OMNY, in 2019 and ended the sales of MetroCard at the close of 2025. However, OMNY issues separate Fair Fares and Reduced-Fare OMNY cards. OMNY could go further than TfL because its subway and bus fare is largely flat so the system never has to pair taps in and out to price a journey by zone, time of day and mode. They have also aligned their weekly payment window to something that resembles the seven-day Travelcard mode. It also helps that New York adopted contactless later, after the bank card model was proven elsewhere, London included.
Paris took a third path and has only really started its contactless journey in 2025. It is used as a fallback for the occasional visitors with higher fares and no connections included; it isn't meant to replace the existing fare options.
So who got it right? None of them is simply better. London made bank cards the default and pushed the older products out to the edges, New York is retiring the MetroCard but still issues its own OMNY cards for concessions, and Paris is keeping its pass ecosystem and adding cards as a safety net. Each one follows from where it started, and London's hybrid is as much a product of its Oyster base and zonal fares as it is of any choice TfL made.
What I take from this is that digitising one interaction doesn't simplify what's behind it. The tap got easier for the passenger, but the rules and products underneath stayed just as complicated (if not have become more complicated). I'd judge a transformation like this less by how simple the front end feels and more by how well it manages the complexity that's left.
4. Why physical change is slow: mobile coverage
One of the best things about the Elizabeth line is that your phone just works. You get 4G and 5G the whole way, with no waiting to reach the next stop or for the train to come above ground. Getting the same on the rest of the Tube has been one of TfL's slowest projects. Trials planned around 2008 stalled in talks with infrastructure providers, and the idea only moved again with a pilot in 2020 and the appointment of a neutral host in 2021. A neutral host means one company builds and runs the underground network and every mobile operator rents space on it, so each doesn't have to put in its own cabling.
The target was the end of 2024. By early 2026 only 62 of the 121 underground stations had coverage, and by October 2026 it was around 70 per cent, with TfL promising to finish by the end of the year. Other cities got there first and London is on track to be the last major peer to finish 4G. Every city went about it slightly differently: Madrid's metro created its own neutral host, while the neutral host building London's network also worked with New York's MTA to build theirs. Credit where it's due, though. Ookla found parts of the Tube now rival the streets above for 5G availability and signal strength.
I'd argue the heart of it is that physical change is hard to undo. The techniques that keep TfL's digital changes safe (pausing, rolling back, running the old and new side by side) don't exist for a cable in a live tunnel. It can't be trialled alongside the old system or easily taken out again, and every installation needs its own access window and safety case. Add old tunnels, overnight maintenance windows, shared space with other equipment, and two leaky-feeder cables in every tunnel for redundancy, and every step is expensive to get wrong. Contactless, by comparison, went from its first buses to the whole network in under two years. Software lets TfL pause and try again. A tunnel doesn't.
5. Staging AI where errors are costly
This wouldn't be a paper written in 2026 if I didn't mention AI. TfL was using machine learning long before generative models took off. By 2018 it already had a model in production on the Central line, predicting when a motor would fail. Whether it paid off is harder to say: the £3 million a year saving was only ever an expectation, and I couldn't find anything that shows the model is still in place. What interests me isn't that TfL uses AI, since every large organisation does, but how it lets it in. The answer is the same as everywhere else in this essay: it keeps each step survivable.
Willesden Green shows the wrapping version. Between October 2022 and September 2023, TfL ran AI over the station's existing CCTV, flagging potential incidents to staff, instead of replacing the cameras. It had its limits: reportedly, the system struggled to detect aggressive behaviour because it had too little training data. New York's MTA took the opposite route with TrackInspect. Instead of working with the records it already had, it generated new data, fitting Google Pixel phones to six A line cars to listen for track defects from inside moving trains. Between September 2024 and January 2025 the phones collected 335 million sensor readings and found 92 per cent of the defects that human inspectors found.
The stakes rise when the AI is watching for people on the track. In December 2025, TfL began a trial on the Central line at West Ruislip, East Acton and Mile End, using existing cameras and new video analytics to detect people falling or entering the track area. It started in a shadow phase, where the system flags incidents without triggering any operational response. Both kinds of errors are costly. A missed event defeats the purpose, while false alarms burden staff and erode trust. New York shows how that plays out. Its 2014 to 2019 track-intrusion pilots could detect an intrusion but, according to the MTA, not precisely enough to manage the response, and its 2024 weapons-detector pilot produced over 100 false alarms in more than 3,000 searches (I myself feel like a one in 30 false alarm for weapons isn’t a bad outcome).
This is where AI fits my argument. It's software, so it can be paused, run in shadow and tested in different lighting and weather before anyone trusts it, which is exactly what a cable in a tunnel can't be. The cost is time. I couldn't find whether TfL's trial has left the shadow phase yet.
6. Conclusion
TfL’s journey to stay modern has not been smooth and I believe this is because it can’t afford to fail so it creates change using steps it can survive. Where a step is cheap and easy to manage, TfL has moved well. Contactless went from its first buses to the whole network in 2 years. Where a step is expensive, TfL is slow. Mobile coverage is still unfinished more than five years after the concession was signed.
Three lessons stand out to me:
Make every step survivable. Deferring commitments, keeping changes small, putting buffers in front of fragile systems and running new things in parallel or in shadow is how TfL changes a system it can't switch off. It's also how it is bringing in AI.
Wrapping has a price. A façade buys flexibility, but the complexity underneath is still there. That's why Oyster still exists and why London's fares are a hybrid.
Physical change can't be done cheaply. The commercial model, the access windows and the safety case need settling before the engineering starts.
None of this makes TfL's record a straightforward success. Surviving a change isn't the same as simplifying a system, and what's left behind has to be paid down eventually. But for a system that can't stop, I'd rather it moved in steps it can survive than leapt and failed.
